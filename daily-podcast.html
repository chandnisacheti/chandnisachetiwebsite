Build log & guide

I built my own morning podcast

Every morning at 5 AM, a 10-minute show about the day’s neurotech and health tech news lands in my Spotify. Two AI hosts walk me through the five stories that matter most to me, the trends that connect them, and which companies are growing. It costs $0, and it runs without me touching anything.

This page has two parts. First, how I built it and what I learned by treating it like a product. Second, a step-by-step guide with the exact prompts, so you can build one for your own field.

  • Part 2: Build your own

Part 1How I built it

Why I built it

At every NYC event I go to, from NeuroNYC happy hours to health tech meetups, the conversation comes back to the same questions. Who just raised? Who got FDA clearance? Which wearable is getting backlash? Where is the field heading?

In neurotech, those answers change every day. So I subscribed to 10 newsletters across medical devices, health tech and neurotech. They were all excellent. Realistically, I wasn’t getting through even one a week.

What I did have was time with my ears free: commuting, walking and running. So I decided to build something that reads the news for me and turns it into a show I’d actually listen to.

Version one: a news dump I forgot in two minutes

I built the first version with Claude. Every morning, a script pulled the last day of news from a list of sources, Gemini wrote a two-host conversation about the top stories, and Gemini’s text-to-speech voiced it. GitHub ran it on a schedule and published it as a podcast feed.

It worked, and it was bad. It crammed in up to eight stories and opened with a rundown of every headline before it got to any of them. I listened on a walk, and two minutes after it ended I couldn’t tell you a single thing I’d heard. The news wasn’t the problem. The format was.

Making it work by ear

Reading and listening are different. When you read, you can skim, jump back, and see the structure. When you listen while doing something else, you get one pass, and you need the show to tell you where you are.

So I rebuilt the structure around how I actually listen:

  • No headline dump. The intro is two lines and one sentence on what’s coming: “Five stories today: three in neurotech, two in health tech.”
  • Numbered stories. “Story two of five.” If my mind wanders, I can find my place again.
  • Named sections. Who expanded (funding, FDA, deals), new research and products, NYC watch, then the wrap-up.
  • One story at a time, each ending on a clear one-line point.
  • A recap at the end that goes back through every story in one sentence each. That recap is what makes it stick.

Crafting Nora and Theo

The first two hosts sounded like the same person reading alternate lines. It felt like listening to a script, not a conversation, and my attention drifted.

So I designed two characters you could tell apart with your eyes closed. Nora is the anchor: quick, bright and curious. She speaks in short sentences, moves the show along, and asks the questions a smart listener would ask, like “Wait, how big is that?” Theo is the engineer: slower, thoughtful, with a dry sense of humor. He explains how the technology actually works and what it means in practice.

Each host gets their own voice and their own speaking style. Nora is voiced to sound bright and energetic, and Theo is lower and more relaxed, with small pauses. I also told the writer to use natural speech: contractions, quick reactions like “Huh” and “Okay, that’s big,” and never the same sentence pattern twice in a row. The first episode with Nora and Theo was the best one yet.

Iterating like a product

For the next few days I treated each episode like a product release. I listened on my morning walk, wrote down what worked and what didn’t, and changed one thing at a time. A few of the changes that made the biggest difference:

  • Fewer, deeper stories. Eight stories was too many to remember. Now it’s five: the three biggest neurotech stories and the two biggest across digital health, medtech and wearables. On a slow or huge day it can flex to four or six.
  • No repeats. The same story kept showing up in two sections. Now each story appears exactly once, and stories already covered in the last three days are skipped.
  • An editor, then a writer. I split the AI into two steps. An editor reads every news item, picks the stories, and writes a log of everything it cut and why. Then a writer turns that plan into the script. That log made the whole thing transparent: I can see exactly what was considered.
  • Teaching the editor my taste. One day the editor cut a story I cared about, a backlash over a well-known founder’s new wearable, calling it an “influencer dispute” and a duplicate. The log showed me why. So I taught it that several outlets covering the same story is a signal it matters, and that controversies over the science behind devices are exactly what I want to hear about. The next episode covered it.

Making it mine

Once the format worked, I added the segments that make it useful for my life specifically:

  • Trends that cut across the day’s stories, so I hear the pattern and not only the headlines.
  • Career Signal, which names the companies that look like they’re growing and what someone with my background in biosignal ML and neuromodulation could bring to each.
  • NYC Watch, because I want to know what’s happening in my own city first.
  • A daily list of companies that raised, expanded or got cleared, which I use to decide who to reach out to next.

Where it is now

Every morning around 5 AM, the pipeline pulls news from more than 20 sources, the editor picks the stories, the writer scripts Nora and Theo, and the episode is voiced and published to my feed, which Spotify picks up. Each episode comes with a full transcript and the editor’s log.

The stack is Claude to help me build it, Gemini to write and voice it, GitHub to run and host it, and Spotify to deliver it. Total cost: $0.

Now I start each day knowing the funding rounds, FDA decisions and research worth talking about, and I walk into rooms with something to say. Everyone worries AI is making us dumber. I’d rather use it to make me smarter.

Part 2Build your own

You don’t need to write code. You’ll copy my project, swap in your topics and prompts, and connect a few free accounts. Plan on about 30 minutes.

You’ll need: a personal Google account (for a free Gemini key), a free GitHub account, and a podcast app.

1. Copy the project

Go to github.com/chandnisacheti/daily-neuro-podcast and click Fork. Keep the repository public, because free GitHub Pages hosting needs a public repository. Then open the Actions tab of your copy and click the button to enable workflows, since GitHub turns them off on copies.

2. Get a free Gemini key

Go to aistudio.google.com/apikey and click Create API key. Use a personal Gmail, because school and work accounts often block AI Studio. Treat the key like a password: don’t paste it into a chat or a file.

3. Store the key in GitHub

In your repository, go to Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → New repository secret. Name it GEMINI_API_KEY, paste the key, and save. Only your workflow can read it.

4. Make it yours

You can edit every file right on GitHub by opening it and clicking the pencil icon.

  • podcast/sources.py is your news list. Each line is a feed. The easiest way to add a topic is a Google News search line, for example google_news('"climate tech" (raises OR launches)'). Delete the neurotech ones you don’t need.
  • podcast/make_episode.py holds the prompts. Replace the editor and writer prompts with your versions of the ones below, and rename the hosts if you like.
  • podcast/feed.py holds the show’s name and owner. Change the title, your name and your email.

5. Turn on hosting

Go to Settings → Pages, choose Deploy from a branch, then pick main and the /docs folder. This is where your episodes and feed will live.

6. Make your first episode

Go to Actions → Daily episode → Run workflow. It takes about 10 to 15 minutes. When it turns green, your feed is at:

https://YOUR-GITHUB-NAME.github.io/daily-neuro-podcast/feed.xml

7. Listen in your podcast app

  • Apple Podcasts: Library → the ⋯ menu → Follow a Show by URL, then paste your feed.
  • Spotify: go to creators.spotify.com, choose to add an existing podcast, paste your feed, and enter the code Spotify emails to the address in feed.py. Spotify only lists public shows, so anyone could find it.

8. Pick your time

The schedule lives in .github/workflows/daily.yml and uses UTC time. 17 9 * * * means 9:17 UTC, which is 4:17 AM in New York in winter. GitHub sometimes runs scheduled jobs late or skips them at busy times, so the project uses an odd minute plus a backup slot that only runs if the first one didn’t.

Good to know

  • Test sparingly. Gemini’s free voices have a daily limit. About six test episodes in one day used mine up. If the limit hits, the project re-voices the episode with free Microsoft Edge voices so you still get a show.
  • Some sites block automated readers. If a source keeps showing up as skipped in the run log, replace it with a Google News search for the same outlet.
  • Read the editor’s log. Each run saves one in docs/logs/. When the show misses a story you wanted, the log tells you why, and that tells you what to change in the prompt.
  • Try the prompts first. Before building anything, paste a few headlines into the Gemini app with the editor prompt, then give it the writer prompt. You’ll hear the format in five minutes.

The promptsExactly what I use

These are the prompts that run my show, with my details swapped for placeholders in [brackets]. Fill those in, and keep {recent}, {items}, {plan} and {date} as they are, because the code fills them in each morning.

One detail if you paste these into make_episode.py: double every curly brace in the JSON example, writing {{ and }}, but leave the four placeholders above single.

The editor

The editor reads every news item and decides what makes the show. Most of your personalization goes here.

You are the editor of "[SHOW NAME]", a daily ~10 minute audio news briefing for [YOUR NAME], [ONE LINE ABOUT YOU: what you do, what you work on, and where you want your career to go].

Pick the stories for today's episode from the numbered news items, and plan the rundown.

Selection rules:
- FIVE main stories by default: the THREE biggest stories in [YOUR CORE TOPIC] and the TWO biggest stories from [YOUR ADJACENT TOPICS]. Go to 4 or 6 only if the day is unusually thin or something huge happened; never more than 6. Pick substantive stories with real depth, because each one gets a full segment.
- Prefer: funding, acquisitions, regulatory decisions, notable research, product launches, [YOUR CITY] companies, and anything that signals hiring or growth.
- When several outlets cover the same story, that is a SIGNAL it matters: merge them into one story (use the best item's id; list the others in skipped as "merged into story N"). Never drop a story just because it has duplicates.
- [YOUR NAME] especially wants: [THE KINDS OF STORIES YOU NEVER WANT TO MISS]. Treat these as strong picks.
- Skip: items not about [YOUR FIELD], thin press releases, shopping guides and generic product reviews, and stories already covered in recent episodes (listed below) unless there is a genuinely new development.
- Each story appears ONCE in the whole episode. Never assign the same story to two sections.
- Choose stories that connect to each other where possible, so the Trends segment has something real to tie together.

Sections are flexible. Default order: "Who Expanded" (funding, deals, approvals, growth), "New Research and Products", "[YOUR CITY] Watch", "Trends", "Career Signal", "Recap". Drop a section if it would only repeat earlier stories. Rename or reorder sections when that makes the day clearer. Trends, Career Signal and Recap tie together stories already told and must not introduce repeats as new stories.

Recently covered stories (do not repeat): {recent}

Return JSON:
{"title": "short episode title",
  "sections": [{"name": "...", "stories": [{"id": 3, "angle": "one line: what to emphasize and why it matters to [YOUR NAME]"}]}],
  "trends": ["2 to 3 trends, each naming which of today's stories (by id) show it and what it means for the field"],
  "career_signal": "3 to 4 concrete lines: which companies or areas look like they are growing or hiring, and the specific role or pitch [YOUR NAME] could make to each",
  "recap": ["exactly one memorable line per main story, in the order they were told"],
  "skipped": [{"id": 7, "reason": "short reason"}]}
Every item id must appear exactly once, either in a section or in skipped.

NEWS ITEMS (JSON):
{items}

The writer

The writer turns the editor’s plan into a conversation. This is where Nora and Theo come from, and where the show becomes easy to follow by ear.

Write the script for today's "[SHOW NAME]" from the editor's plan below.
Today is {date}. Listener: [YOUR NAME] (see plan). They listen while walking or commuting.

HOSTS (distinct people, distinct ways of talking):
- Nora: the anchor. Quick, bright, curious. Short punchy sentences, asks the questions a smart listener would ask ("Wait, how big is that?"), moves the show along, announces sections.
- Theo: the engineer-analyst. Slower, thoughtful, a little dry humor. Explains the mechanism or the "so what" in plain words, often starts with "Here's the thing" or "So, practically..." (vary it).
They trade off who introduces stories. Natural speech: contractions, varied sentence length, quick reactions ("Huh.", "Right.", "Okay, that's big."), occasional friendly back-and-forth. Never robotic, never the same sentence pattern twice in a row. Do not say "This matters because" or "The takeaway" every time; vary how you land each story, but every story should end with a clear one-line point.

EASY TO FOLLOW BY EAR:
- Intro: two short lines (hello, the date), plus one line saying how many stories are coming ("Five stories today: three in [CORE TOPIC], two in [ADJACENT TOPICS]"). No list of headlines up front.
- Number the stories as you go ("Story two of five"), so the listener always knows where they are.
- Announce each section clearly by name, e.g. "Okay, first up: who expanded this week." Signal transitions ("That's the deals. Now, research.").
- One story at a time: who, what happened, why it matters. Spell numbers the way people say them.
- Follow the plan's sections and order exactly; each story is told once.
- Give each main story a full segment (about 150 to 200 words): what happened, how it works, why it matters, and a clear one-line point at the end.
- The closing segments are what make the episode memorable, so make them strong (about 350 words total):
  * Trends: two or three patterns that cut ACROSS today's stories. For each, name the stories that show it, then what it means for where the field is heading.
  * Career Signal: concrete and specific. Which companies or areas are growing, and for each one, what role or pitch someone with [YOUR BACKGROUND] could bring them.
  * Recap: go back through every main story in order, one memorable sentence each ("One: ... Two: ..."), then one sentence that ties the whole day together.
- Use only facts present in the items. Do not invent numbers, names or quotes.
- About 1,500 to 1,700 words.

Output ONLY dialogue lines, each starting with "Nora:" or "Theo:". No stage directions, no markdown.

EDITOR'S PLAN (JSON):
{plan}

ITEMS USED (JSON):
{items}
